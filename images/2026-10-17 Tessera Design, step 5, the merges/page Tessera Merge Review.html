Merge review

Each card shows the whole paragraphs that say the same thing today, under the name of their document, with the repeated parts highlighted. Below them is how each document would read after the merge, and under each one the field that will hold each part, what that field is for, the record and what changes. The highlighted text is then stored once, and every document prints it.

How to answer: reply in the chat with the numbers, for example "all yes, Q2 b, Q3 later". You can also change a wording.

repeated text (Today) · text stored once (Proposed) after the merge Dashed box: your decision in the app

The fields (written table.field) and what each one is for:

  • blocks.body: a piece of design text in a doc. Several docs can print the same block; it is then written once.
  • features.input, features.behaviour: a feature’s “Input” and “Expected behaviour/output”. They tell the programmer what the game must do, and the M1 TDD prints them.
  • decisions.decision: one of your decisions, word for word. Docs print only its text; the date and why stay in the app.
  • open_questions.question: one undecided question. Every doc that lists it prints the same record, so answering it once answers it everywhere.
  • milestones.purpose: a milestone’s “What it’s for”.

Under each proposed text, the “What it’s for” column says what that record does in its doc. Where a decision or question now prints in the middle of a block, the block is split in two at that point; the text of both parts stays the same.

M3a Orientation

Today
22. Controls and camera
Orientation decided for M1/M2: landscape. This matches Sky: Children of the Light — the closest existing comparable in both mechanics (no combat, restoration-driven exploration) and design goal (calm, low-pressure) — and wider field of view has a measurably significant effect on presence/immersion (Cummings & Bailenson, 2016), relevant to Tessera's atmosphere/scenery-as-reward direction. Portrait is not ruled out — it's deferred and re-explored at M3, whether specifically for the real-world layer or more broadly. Meditation/wellness/habit apps generally favor portrait for quick one-handed check-ins, a different interaction pattern from the core game's — worth checking specifically against Kinder World once its orientation is confirmed, since it's the closest existing comparable that combines a game with a real-world wellbeing layer. Supporting both orientations at once still isn't assumed at any stage — it adds UI/camera/testing scope regardless of when it's introduced.
Your decisions, 29 September

17. Research Methods
=== Research plan, in order: . Literature review — complete (above). . Qualitative interviews: two separate blind groups, each reacting to only half of the concept — one group to the game loop alone, one to the real-world layer alone — so no single participant ever sees the full idea. Each interview opens with questions about the person's own relaxation and habit routines before the concept comes up at all, so answers aren't primed. Around 12 interviews per group (24 total) is enough for the main themes to stop changing and start repeating. . Quantitative survey: an unbranded, disguised version of the concept, split so each respondent sees only one version — the game alone, the game with the optional real-world layer, or a real, already-successful comparable game disguised the same way. Showing only one version each, rather than several back-to-back, stops an attentive respondent from noticing that two of the versions are secretly related. Around 200 completed responses per version. Ask appeal, purchase interest, and a forced ranking against other games, rather than relying on "would you buy this" alone, which is an unreliable single question for concepts nobody has seen before. Add a standard four-question pricing exercise and a forced best/worst exercise on five or six ways of describing the real-world layer, to find which framing reads as motivating rather than gimmicky — a simple "which do you prefer" question tends to make everything look equally liked. . Empirical smoke test: small, short-lived disguised ad campaigns using only existing concept art and a placeholder project name — never the real title or studio name — linking to two simple pages: one describing the game alone, one describing the game with the optional layer. The tracked action is signing up for updates, not a purchase, so this measures real behaviour instead of survey answers. At least 1,000 ad views per version before the click-through numbers mean anything; a rate around 2% or higher on either page is a good sign, and anything under 1% is a warning sign regardless of what the interviews or survey said. . Direct comparison against real similar products already on the market, to keep every result grounded in reality rather than wishful thinking. .
